In the headset: a cello stands in front of you. Your left hand stops the strings: hold the left trigger (or pinch) with your hand on the neck — higher up the neck is higher pitch, like the real thing. Your right hand holds the bow: draw it across the strings near the bridge. Bow speed is loudness, the right trigger adds weight, and tilting the bow picks the string.

Easy mode (default on): the bow sounds whenever the right trigger is held and you move your hand, so you don't need to hit the strings exactly. A = next articulation, B = loop rec/overdub, X = drone, Y = recenter the cello, thumbstick = dynamics. Hand tracking works too: pinch to press.

Learnten short lessons: how a cello works and how to play this one

Each lesson has a Try it button that sets things up. Read, try, change one thing, listen again.

1 · Four strings in fifths

A cello's strings are tuned C2 G2 D3 A3 — each a perfect fifth (seven semitones) above the last, an octave below a viola. The fingerboard shows them left to right (bottom to top in landscape), lowest first, with the nut at the top/left. The open-string names sit by the nut.

This isn't a recording. Each note is a digital waveguide: a simulation of waves running up and down a string, with a bow that grips and slips (the “stick–slip” or Helmholtz motion) — once per cycle, 65 times a second on the open C.

2 · The bow: speed, weight and where it sits

Three things shape a bowed note: bow speed (mostly loudness — the Dynamics slider, or velocity), bow pressure (how hard it presses: too little and the tone goes airy, too much and it crunches) and the contact point. Near the bridge (sul ponticello) the tone is bright and glassy; over the fingerboard (sul tasto) it's soft and flutey.

Try: hold a note, then drag around the bow pad — left/right is contact point, up/down is pressure. Then switch on Hand bow and stroke the pad back and forth while you hold a note: now you are the bow.

3 · The left hand: intonation, vibrato, slides

A cello has no frets, so the player's finger decides the exact pitch (intonation). Vibrato is the finger rocking back and forth — usually 5–7 times a second, a few cents wide. A glissando is a slide between notes.

Try: set Snap to fretless, find a note by ear, then wiggle your finger. Then set Snap back to soft: still fingers get pulled into tune, but your wiggle stays.

4 · Articulations

Arco is normal bowing. Spiccato bounces the bow for short notes. Pizzicato plucks with a finger. Tremolo moves the bow back and forth as fast as possible — the sound of suspense in film scores. Col legno hits the string with the wood of the bow. Harmonics touch the string lightly so it vibrates in halves: a pure, whistling note an octave up. Number keys 1–9 switch between them.

5 · Double stops and the section

Bowing two strings at once is a double stop. Because the strings are a fifth apart, fifths are the most natural — and a fifth is exactly a guitarist's power chord. Thirds and sixths (the “in key” options) follow the scale you pick in Sound.

Players turns one cello into a duet, a quartet or a section of eight. Each player is slightly out of tune and out of time with the others — that spread is what makes a section sound big.

6 · Chugging: the cello goes metal

Cellos have played metal for decades — Apocalyptica's debut, Plays Metallica by Four Cellos, came out in 1996. The Chug articulation plays short, hard strokes locked to the tempo and pushes the string through the Chungus amp: a tightening filter before a valve-style clipper, then a speaker-cabinet filter. Hold a note and it gallops. Pick the rhythm under Sound → Chug rhythm; add a fifth double stop for power chords.

7 · Building a piece with the looper

Live looping means recording a short passage, letting it repeat, and stacking more parts on top. Cellist Zoë Keating builds whole pieces this way. A classic plan: a bass line first, then harmony, then the melody. Leave Length on free and the first loop sets the tempo; pick bars to lock it to the metronome with a count-in.

Every layer can be muted, turned down, reversed or played at half speed (an octave down, twice as long). Try: load the Pachelbel bass into the looper, then play over it.

8 · Make an instrument out of anything

This is the MeowSynth trick: record a sound, find its pitch, and play it back faster or slower to hit every other note. Open Voice & mic, record a steady “chuuug” or a hum into a slot (or borrow a meow), and set Sound source to sampler or both. The sampler finds a loop inside your sound so held notes keep going.

9 · Tuning, pitch standards and scordatura

Modern orchestras tune A4 to around 440 Hz; many baroque ensembles use 415 Hz, about a semitone lower. Scordatura means retuning strings for a piece: Bach's fifth cello suite asks for the A string to be tuned down to G, and Kodály's solo sonata lowers the bottom two strings to B and F♯. Both are in Sound → Tuning, and the sympathetic strings retune with them.

10 · Playing it for real: phones, MIDI, VR

Phones: turn the phone sideways for longer strings; use two hands — one on the fingerboard, one stroking the bow pad in Hand bow mode. Latency (the delay between touch and sound) is lowest with wired headphones; Bluetooth adds a noticeable lag, which the looper compensates for automatically. MIDI: an MPE controller gives every finger its own slide, pressure and bow position. VR: on a Quest, open the VR drawer and press Enter VR.
